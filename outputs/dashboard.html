IN PLAIN WORDS

Every night, survey telescopes flag possible new near-Earth asteroids and post them to the NEO Confirmation Page. Astronomers must observe them again within days, or their orbits become too uncertain and they are lost. Only about half turn out to be real; the rest are camera artifacts, satellites, stars or ordinary main-belt asteroids.

The ranking puts the likeliest real ones first, so scarce telescope time goes where it matters. P(real NEO) is the chance a candidate is a genuine near-Earth object, not the chance of hitting Earth. No candidate here has an impact assessment; known impact risks are in NASA's Sentry table further down.

BEFORE YOU ACT ON THIS RANKING

  • It ranks better than the digest2 score alone (ROC-AUC 0.91 vs 0.72, tested on 377 candidates posted after 2026-10-01, trained on 1131 earlier ones), but at the strict setting that keeps ≥99% of real NEOs it drops only 37% of junk (digest2 alone: 27%).
  • Probabilities are not calibrated: use them to order follow-up, not as odds.
  • Labels are noisy: some candidates marked “not confirmed” were real NEOs that were simply lost.
  • Trained on 1508 candidates (last retrained 19 Oct 2026; retrained every Monday); unusual candidates unlike past ones may be scored poorly.
  • Not an impact forecast: a high score means “probably a real near-Earth object”, nothing about hitting Earth.

EVALUATION · ON CANDIDATES IT NEVER SAW

Is the model any good?

Yes, at ranking: on candidates it had never seen, posted after it was trained, it ranked real near-Earth objects above junk better than the digest2 score alone (ROC-AUC 0.91 vs 0.72). At a cut-off that keeps 95% of real ones, it threw out 62% of the junk; digest2 alone threw out 41%.

THE ERRORS · WHERE THE RANKING GOES WRONG

Investigate the errors

10real near-Earth objects the model would have dropped on the later test candidates, at a cut-off meant to keep 95% of them. Each one is a discovery delayed or lost.
59junk candidates it would still have sent for follow-up at that cut-off, each one wasted telescope time.
57%of past candidates that digest2 scored 90 or more turned out to be real near-Earth objects. A high score is a hint, not a verdict, for any method.

Why the ranking fails: labels are noisy ("never confirmed" includes real objects nobody caught in time), unusual candidates look like nothing it has seen, and its probabilities are not calibrated. The full analysis is on the evidence page.
