THE SCIENCE OF DETECTION · EVIDENCE

What we found, and how sure we are.

SpaceSift asks one question of three kinds of sky data: when a search says “found it”, how often is it right, and what does it miss? Each investigation below states its data, method and baseline, counts its errors in both directions, names its limits, and shows how to rerun it.

WHAT WE BUILT

An injection-recovery pipeline that has run 72,440 planet searches with known answers; a star classifier matched against 123 real spectra; a live ranking of new asteroid candidates.

WHAT'S NEW

Measuring failure, not just success: completeness maps across stellar variability, with each cleaning method's threshold calibrated on noise-only stars, and a pre-registered test on real Kepler variables (predictions committed before the run).

THE EVIDENCE

On pulsating stars the standard filter found 0 of 106 planets; removing the rhythm first found 105. The star classifier's median error is 1 subclass (old method: 5). The asteroid model ranks better than digest2 but doesn't yet beat it where it matters most.

Every experiment records its code commit, configuration and random seed. Commands for each are below; the code is on GitHub.

INVESTIGATION 01 · HIDDEN PLANETS

When does a transit search miss a planet that is really there?

Open the Transit Lab →
CLEAR RESULTOn quiet stars the standard search behaves as published. On stars that pulsate at 100 ppm or more, no amount of filtering finds the planets, but removing the star's rhythm first recovers 99% or more, in all 24 pulsation settings tested. The reverse holds for fast-spinning spotted stars. No single cleaning method works on every star.

IN PLAIN WORDS

This is a detection curve for quiet stars. A planet whose dips stand 5× above the noise is almost never found; at about 11× it's a coin flip; above 16× the search nearly always finds it.

It matches published results, which shows the pipeline works before we test the hard cases.

IN PLAIN WORDS

On flickering stars the standard filter found the planet in just over a third of trials. Most misses weren't “too faint”: the search found the star's own flicker and mistook it for a planet.

Removing the star's rhythm first lifts recovery to 86% across every setting, and to 99% or more on pulsating stars.

ERRORS IN BOTH DIRECTIONS

  • False alarms (false positives): each method's detection threshold is set so only 1% of searches on noise-only stars beat it (1,800 and 10,800 null searches). But on variable stars where cleaning fails, 60–100% of planet-free searches beat the threshold: the false-alarm rate is only valid on quiet stars.
  • Misses (false negatives): half of planets at SNR 10.85 are missed on quiet stars, and 4% of strong signals (SNR > 16) are still missed. On two pulsating Kepler stars in SS-0001, all 100 planted planets were missed.

LIMITATIONS

  • Simulated pulsators are two constant sine waves, the ideal case for prewhitening; noise is white. SS-0002B tests real stars, with predictions committed before the run.
  • BLS measures a box-shaped depth, so recovered transits are ~15% shallower than injected (explained in SS-0001-depth).
  • The in-browser Transit Lab is a JavaScript port checked against the Python pipeline; one demo run is an example, not a result.
Full methodology: data, method, baselines, detailed results and how to rerun

DATA

200 quiet Kepler stars (SS-0001; NASA MAST light curves) and 820 simulated stars spanning pulsation and starspot periods and amplitudes (SS-0002A). 316 real variable Kepler stars for the pre-registered test (SS-0002B, running).

METHOD

Plant planets of known size and orbit, clean the light curve, run a box least-squares (BLS) search, and grade each trial: found, wrong period, the star's own signal, or below threshold. Completeness is fit against signal-to-noise.

BASELINE

The standard pipeline: a 1-day sliding biweight filter, compared against shorter and longer windows, no cleaning, and prewhitening (subtracting the star's rhythms) before filtering.

INVESTIGATION 02 · STARLIGHT

Can a star's type be read reliably from any spectrum someone uploads?

Open the Spectrum Lab →
WORKS, WITH KNOWN GAPSLoading…

IN PLAIN WORDS

Each row is one real star. Blue dots (the current method) mostly sit within a few subclasses of the right answer. Orange dots (our first method) are often off by a whole class or more, calling an A star a G star, for example.

IN PLAIN WORDS

Real spectra often have the wrong overall slope, from dust or calibration. Judging temperature by color alone gets fooled badly. Reading the absorption lines instead barely changes the answer.

ERRORS IN BOTH DIRECTIONS

  • Too hot: faint SDSS “A0” and “B9” stars come out as B stars (5 and 6.5 subclasses off). They are probably blue horizontal-branch stars, old and metal-poor, which the library lacks.
  • Too cool: rare here; the G2 star comes out F8 (too hot by 4) and K7 comes out K5.

LIMITATIONS

  • Small test set (11 stars), one per class; “truth” is itself SDSS's template fit, not an independent measurement.
  • The library is thin on red dwarfs and has no blue horizontal-branch stars. Temperatures come from the matched type (±a few hundred K); metal content is not measured.
  • Pictures of spectra are matched on line positions only, since drawings don't show true line strengths.
Full methodology: data, method, baselines, detailed results and how to rerun

DATA

Templates: 123 real stellar spectra (SDSS spEigenStar-55734, mostly the Indo-US library). Test set: the highest signal-to-noise SDSS DR17 star of each class, O to M (11 stars), chosen by a fixed query before tuning.

METHOD

Fit every template times a smooth curve (absorbing flux calibration and dust), weighting the features astronomers classify by. Errors are counted in subclasses against SDSS's own pipeline type (10 subclasses = one class).

BASELINES

(1) The lab's first classifier: a simplified physics model (blackbody plus ~40 lines). (2) “Temperature from color”: the same templates using only the overall continuum shape.

node --test spectra/test/            # parsers, template library, picture reader, known stars

Results file: spectra/eval/results.json. Templates are rebuilt from the SDSS file with spectra/tools/build_templates.mjs.

INVESTIGATION 03 · NEW ASTEROIDS

Which unconfirmed candidates are real near-Earth objects?

Open the live dashboard →
PROMISING, NOT YET PROVENLoading…

WHAT THE PROBABILITY MEANS, AND DOESN'T

  • P(real NEO) is the chance that a candidate on the Minor Planet Center's confirmation page turns out to be a genuine near-Earth object, rather than junk (artifacts, satellites, stars, lost objects) or an ordinary main-belt asteroid.
  • It is not an impact probability. Nothing in this model estimates whether anything will hit Earth. Impact probabilities for known objects come from NASA JPL's Sentry system and are shown separately on the dashboard.

IN PLAIN WORDS

Pick one real NEO and one non-NEO at random: how often does the model score the real one higher? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

IN PLAIN WORDS

Observers need to drop as much junk as possible without losing real NEOs.

ERRORS IN BOTH DIRECTIONS

    LIMITATIONS

    • Labels are noisy: “not confirmed” includes some real NEOs that were simply lost; “designated other” is assumed not to be an NEO.
    • Few junk examples: most negatives are ordinary main-belt asteroids, and the MPC deletes tracklets of unconfirmed objects, so the hardest junk can't be backfilled.
    • Small test set (about a hundred candidates per split), so differences have wide error bars.
    • Probabilities are not calibrated (class-balanced training); treat them as a ranking.
    Full methodology: data, method, baselines, detailed results and how to rerun

    DATA

    Twice-daily captures of the MPC confirmation page since July 2026 (3,326 resolved outcomes). Labels from the MPC's Previous-NEOCP list: designated NEO = 1; not confirmed, not real, artificial, star or main-belt = 0.

    METHOD

    Gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM DART, 67 features: digest2 orbit-class scores, motion, brightness, sky position, observatory, and changes across snapshots). Tested on a time split: trained on older candidates, scored once on newer ones.

    BASELINE

    The digest2 score alone, the standard tool observers already use to judge whether a candidate looks like a near-Earth object.
