Most stars are made of nearly the same stuff: about 74% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, with everything else making up the last 1 to 2%. Asteroids don't make their own light; they reflect sunlight. Different rocks absorb different colors, especially in the infrared near 1 and 2 microns. Comparing the reflected light to sunlight tells you what kind of rock the asteroid is. That matters for planetary defense: a solid stony asteroid and a loose carbon-rich rubble pile react very differently to being pushed.

What a spectrum is

A spectrum is light spread out by color (wavelength), like a prism making a rainbow. Every atom and molecule absorbs light only at its own set of wavelengths, so dark lines in a spectrum work like fingerprints. Hydrogen always absorbs at 656.3 nm, sodium at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, and so on.

Stars: temperature first, ingredients second

The overall color of a star comes from its temperature. Hot stars glow blue, cool stars red, following the blackbody (Planck) curve; Wien's law gives the peak color. The pattern of absorption lines comes mostly from temperature too, because heat decides which electrons are in place to absorb visible light. That is why astronomers sort stars into the O B A F G K M sequence by their lines.

Once the temperature is known, line depths give the composition. The standard measure is iron, written [Fe/H]: 0 means the Sun's share of iron, −1 means a tenth of it, +0.3 means double.

Why it matters for planets: a transit tells you how big a planet is compared with its star. To turn that into kilometers you need the star's size, which comes from its spectrum. Metal-rich stars are also more likely to host giant planets (Fischer & Valenti 2005).

Asteroids: reflected sunlight

Asteroids are seen by reflected sunlight, so astronomers divide their spectrum by the Sun's to get reflectance: how much of each color the surface bounces back. Silicate minerals carve absorption bands near 1 µm (olivine and pyroxene) and 2 µm (pyroxene). Carbon-rich surfaces are dark and nearly flat. Organic-rich outer-solar-system bodies are very red. These shapes define the Bus-DeMeo classes (DeMeo et al. 2009), and samples brought back by Hayabusa (Itokawa), Hayabusa2 (Ryugu) and OSIRIS-REx (Bennu) have confirmed what the classes mean.

Why it matters for planetary defense: composition hints at density and structure. Whether an asteroid is solid rock or a loose rubble pile changes how much a spacecraft impact moves it; the DART mission showed that debris thrown off a target can multiply the push.

What this lab simplifies

  • Star spectra are a teaching model: a blackbody times a few dozen of the strongest lines, with strengths that follow the real temperature pattern. Real spectra have thousands of lines, and gravity, rotation and magnetic fields also shape them.
  • Spectral class comes from temperature alone. Real classification also uses a luminosity class (dwarf, giant, supergiant), which is not modeled here.
  • Asteroid curves are smooth templates that capture each class's shape, not the averaged class spectra from DeMeo et al. 2009. Colors from them use filter centers only.
  • The color rule (Ivezić et al. 2001) gives a rough group, not a class. Many asteroids sit near the boundaries.
