What a spectrum is

A spectrum is light spread out by color (wavelength), like a prism making a rainbow. Every atom and molecule absorbs light only at its own set of wavelengths, so dark lines work like fingerprints. Hydrogen always absorbs at 656.3 nm, sodium at 589.0 and 589.6 nm, and so on.

Stars: temperature first, ingredients second

A star's overall color comes from its temperature: hot stars glow blue, cool stars red (the blackbody or Planck curve). The pattern of lines comes mostly from temperature too, because heat decides which atoms are in a state to absorb visible light. That's why astronomers sort stars into the O B A F G K M sequence by their lines.

Once the temperature is known, line depths give the composition. The standard measure is iron, written [Fe/H]: 0 means the Sun's share of iron, −1 a tenth of it, +0.3 double.

Why it matters for planets: a transit tells you a planet's size compared with its star. Turning that into kilometers needs the star's size, which comes from its spectrum. Metal-rich stars are also more likely to host giant planets (Fischer & Valenti 2005).

Asteroids: reflected sunlight

Asteroids are seen by reflected sunlight, so astronomers divide their spectrum by the Sun's to get reflectance. Silicate minerals make dips near 1 µm (olivine and pyroxene) and 2 µm (pyroxene). Carbon-rich surfaces are dark and nearly flat. Organic-rich outer-solar-system bodies are very red. These shapes define the Bus-DeMeo classes (DeMeo et al. 2009), and samples returned by Hayabusa (Itokawa), Hayabusa2 (Ryugu) and OSIRIS-REx (Bennu) confirmed what the classes mean.

Why it matters for planetary defense: composition hints at density and structure. Whether an asteroid is solid rock or a loose rubble pile changes how much a spacecraft impact moves it; NASA's DART mission showed that debris thrown off the target can multiply the push.

What this lab simplifies

  • Star spectra are a teaching model: a blackbody times a few dozen of the strongest lines, with strengths that follow the real temperature pattern. Real spectra have thousands of lines, and gravity, rotation and magnetic fields also shape them.
  • Spectral class comes from temperature alone; real classification also uses a luminosity class (dwarf, giant, supergiant).
  • Asteroid curves are smooth templates that capture each class's shape, not averaged survey spectra. Colors from them use filter centers only.
  • The color rule (Ivezić et al. 2001) gives a rough group, not a class. Many asteroids sit near the boundaries.
