Dark Matter Detective

How it works

Stars far from a galaxy's centre should orbit slowly, the way Neptune orbits slower than Earth. In real galaxies they don't. Can the matter we see explain how 175 real galaxies spin, and if not, how much is missing?

STEP 1 · INSPECT THE DATA

How fast does a galaxy spin?

Radio telescopes measure the Doppler shift of hydrogen gas across a galaxy's disk. That gives the speed at which material orbits at each distance from the centre: the galaxy's rotation curve. Every point here is a real measurement.

Rotation curve

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

If most of a galaxy's mass sits where its light is, near the centre, then far out the speed should fall with distance, as v ∝ 1/√r, just like the planets. Does it?

The dashed curves are the speeds the visible matter alone would produce: gas (from its radio emission) and stars (from infrared light). How much mass the stars have per unit of light, the mass-to-light ratio Υ, is uncertain, so it's your dial. Stellar-population models put it near 0.5 at this wavelength. STEP 3 · REVEAL THE EVIDENCE

Fit both explanations

Model A uses visible matter only, with Υ free to take whatever value fits best, even impossibly high ones. Model B fixes the stars at Υ = 0.5 and adds a dark halo: a sphere of unseen mass whose density and size are fitted. The fit quality is reduced χ²: about 1 means the model matches within the measurement errors.

WHERE THE HALO MODEL FAILS TOO

LIMITS OF THIS LAB

  • Rotation curves show that mass is missing, not what it is. The case for dark matter being a new kind of particle comes from other evidence too: the cosmic microwave background, galaxy clusters such as the Bullet Cluster, and gravitational lensing.
  • Modified gravity is a live alternative for galaxies. MOND changes the law of gravity below about 10−10 m/s² and fits rotation curves well. The tight relation in the last chart is what it predicts. It struggles with clusters and the cosmic microwave background.
  • One halo shape. The cored isothermal halo is a convenient choice. The NFW profile from cosmological simulations is steeper in the centre and fits many dwarf galaxies worse: the "cusp–core" problem.
  • Errors not included. Uncertainties in distance and inclination, gas pressure and non-circular motions near the centre are not propagated. Some poor fits come from these, not from the models.
HOW IT WORKS

The physics and the data

Orbits weigh mass

For a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force, so the speed at radius r measures the mass inside it:

v² / r = G M(<r) / r² → M(<r) = v² r / G

Visible components add in quadrature: Vbar² = Vgas² + ΥdiskVdisk² + ΥbulgeVbulge², with Υbulge = 1.4 Υdisk.

The dark halo

A pseudo-isothermal sphere, ρ(r) = ρ₀ / (1 + (r/rc)²), gives

Vhalo² = 4πGρ₀rc² [1 − (rc/r) arctan(r/rc)]

which rises and then flattens: exactly the shape the visible matter can't produce. Fits search ρ₀ and rc on a grid to minimise χ².

The data

SPARC: 175 disk galaxies with Spitzer 3.6 μm photometry and HI/Hα rotation curves. Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016, AJ 152, 157. Radial acceleration relation: McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert 2016, PRL 117, 201101.

The first flat rotation curves: Rubin, Ford & Thonnard 1980. NGC 3198's classic analysis: van Albada et al. 1985.
