How it works

When two black holes spiral together they shake spacetime, and LIGO feels it as a stretch smaller than a proton. Can you find the collision buried in the noise, and how often does the search get it wrong?

STEP 1 · INSPECT THE DATA

Four seconds from a detector

Each stretch of data is simulated detector noise. Some stretches also hide the chirp of two black holes merging. Pick one to start.

Detector strain (whitened)

Strain is how much the detector's arms stretch, divided by their length. The data are whitened: rescaled so noise at every frequency has the same strength, which is how LIGO's searches see it. One unit on this axis is the typical noise level.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

A merger is a chirp: a wave that rises in both pitch and loudness over a second or two, then stops when the black holes merge. Only the loudest chirps are visible by eye. Try smoothing the data. Can you see anything?

CHIRP MASS

The chirp mass Mc = (m1m2)3/5/(m1+m2)1/5 sets how fast the pitch rises. Lighter pairs chirp slowly and spend longer in the detector's band. Heavier pairs sweep up in a fraction of a second and merge at a lower pitch. Two 35 M☉ black holes have Mc ≈ 30 M☉.

STEP 3 · REVEAL THE EVIDENCE

Run the full template search

The search tries 48 templates from 15 to 60 M☉ at every start time and keeps the loudest match. It reports a detection when that SNR passes 5.4, the level that pure noise passes in only 1% of stretches (calibrated on 2,000 noise-only stretches).

STEP 4 · INVESTIGATE THE ERRORS

One search proves nothing. Run 600.

The only way to know how good a search is: hide signals you know about and count how often it finds them, and run it on pure noise and count how often it is fooled. LIGO does this with millions of fake signals. This runs 200 noise-only stretches and 400 with a hidden merger at SNR 3 to 12, right in your browser.

LIMITS OF THIS LAB

  • Simulated, idealised noise. Real detector noise has glitches: short bursts that can mimic chirps. That is why LIGO demands SNR around 8 or more and checks two detectors at once, not 5.4 in one.
  • Inspiral only. Templates use the leading-order (Newtonian) chirp and stop at the last stable orbit. Real templates include higher-order relativity, spin, unequal masses, the merger and the ringdown.
  • One detector, 4 seconds. False-alarm rates here are per 4-second stretch. LIGO quotes them per year of data, which needs much higher thresholds.
  • Not real LIGO data (yet). The loud preset is modelled on GW150914 (network SNR 24, chirp mass about 30 M☉), but the noise is simulated.
HOW IT WORKS

The physics and the search

The chirp

General relativity predicts that an orbiting pair radiates gravitational waves at twice its orbital frequency and loses energy, so it spirals in. To leading order, with τ the time left before merger and T = GMc/c³:

f(τ) = (1/π) · (5 / 256τ)3/8 · T−5/8

The amplitude grows as f2/3. The signal ends near the innermost stable orbit, fISCO = c³ / (63/2πGM), about 64 Hz for a 69 M☉ pair.

The matched filter

For known signal shapes in Gaussian noise, the best possible detector is to correlate the data with a template of the expected signal. The SNR at each start time is

ρ(t₀) = |⟨d, h(t − t₀)⟩| / ‖h‖

using two copies of the template a quarter-cycle apart, so the unknown phase doesn't matter. It is computed for every start time at once with a Fourier transform. A signal hidden at optimal SNR ρ gives a peak near ρ.

Same idea as finding planets

This is the same problem as SpaceSift's Transit Lab: a repeating box-shaped dip there, a chirp here, both buried in noise and found by sliding a template across the data. Both are judged the same way: a threshold set from noise alone, then completeness measured with signals of known strength.
