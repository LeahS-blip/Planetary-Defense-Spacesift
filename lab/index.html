What is this?

Astronomers find planets around other stars by watching for tiny, repeating dips in a star's brightness as a planet passes in front. Search programs hunt for those dips. The Transit Lab asks a different question from "can it find planets?": when, and why, does the search miss them?

To answer that, SpaceSift plants fake planets of known size and orbit in real and simulated light curves, runs the search, and counts how often it succeeds, like hiding eggs and grading the searcher.

What it has found so far

SS-0001. On 200 quiet Kepler stars, the standard search (box least squares) recovers half of planted planets at a signal-to-noise of 10.9 and 97% at high signal. The pipeline behaves as expected.
SS-0002A (simulation). Stellar variability can hide planets entirely. On a star pulsating every 4 hours at 100 ppm, the standard cleaning step found 0 of 106 planted planets; removing the pulsation's rhythm first (prewhitening) found 105. For fast-spinning spotted stars it is the reverse: prewhitening fails and only a short cleaning window helps. No single method works on every star.
SS-0002B (real stars, in progress). Whether the simulated map predicts what happens on 316 real variable Kepler stars. Predictions were committed before the run.

How the demo relates to the experiments

The experiments run the Python pipeline on many thousands of planted planets. This page runs a JavaScript version of the same steps in your browser, checked against the Python one; its detection threshold is calibrated separately (1% false alarms on noise-only stars), so its numbers can differ slightly from the experiments'. One run is one example, not a result.
