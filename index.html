Every night, telescopes record millions of points of light. Most are noise. Some are new worlds. A few could come close to Earth. SpaceSift asks how often we catch them, and how often we are fooled.

INVESTIGATION 01 · HIDDEN PLANETS

Worlds hide in starlight.

When a planet crosses in front of its star, the starlight dips. For an Earth passing a Sun-like star, by less than 0.01%.

INVESTIGATION 01 · HIDDEN PLANETS

Searches hunt for the repeat.

Planet-hunting software scans months of brightness for a dip that comes back, orbit after orbit.

INVESTIGATION 01 · HIDDEN PLANETS

But stars flicker.

Pulsations and starspots can drown the signal. In one simulated scenario, a star pulsating every 4 hours, the standard search found 0 of 106 hidden planets, and 105 once the star's rhythm was removed.

Calcium, hydrogen, sodium, iron. Their pattern reveals how hot a star is and what it is made of, and the same trick tells rock from metal on an asteroid.

WHAT IT IS

Three free tools and the open experiments behind them: a lab for hiding planets and testing the search, a lab for reading what stars and asteroids are made of, and a live ranking of new asteroid candidates.

WHO IT'S FOR

Students, observers and anyone curious how we know what's out there, not just the headline results. Every number comes from code and data you can rerun.

WHY IT MATTERS

A missed planet looks exactly like no planet, so counts of how common worlds are depend on knowing what searches miss. And a new asteroid that isn't followed up in time can be lost.
